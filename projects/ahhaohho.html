Project 01 · Children's Creative Learning

Fostering Curiosity
Through Creative Learning

Creative Learning Platform · Smilegate FutureLab Foundation

AhhaOhho is a creative learning platform where children discover hands-on activities, express ideas through tinkering, and grow through shared projects and peer feedback. I led product design at Smilegate FutureLab Foundation, working with one PM, four engineers, and two designers. I designed the research program and the gamification system myself, and art-directed the character illustration with an external partner. During field research I tested with 1,000+ children. V1.0 launched in June 2026 and now reaches 3,000+ children users, with a 12% Day 7 retention.

The Problem

Children have the devices. What they lack is a space to be curious.

In Korea, academic pressure starts early. Most parents and students focus heavily on standardized testing from elementary school onward — leaving little room for open-ended exploration or creative risk-taking. Over 90% of 6th graders own smartphones, but for most children these devices default to passive content consumption rather than hands-on expression.

AhhaOhho is a creative learning platform that encourages children to discover making activities, share their own projects, and grow through peer feedback.

02

User Research

Field studies across schools, community centers, and a pop-up at the Busan International Kids & Youth Film Festival : 500+ children, 15 facilitators, and 8 workshop programs.

Concept Board

User Personas

Key Findings

Sharing mattered more than making.

Students were motivated less by the activities themselves and more by the social layer around them (e.g. seeing peers' work, leaving reactions, getting noticed.) At one school, the teacher chose a competitor specifically for its community sharing. This told me the engagement spine had to be social, not content.

Every classroom bent the app to fit. None used it as designed.

Teachers repurposed challenges, swapped in external tools for sharing, and compressed the app's role to a brief intro. The creative loop we designed wasn't wrong, but the platform forced teachers to complete it outside the app. The full cycle needed to live in one place.

Device access didn't mean interface fluency.

App crashes on older tablets, text-heavy screens that lost kids' attention, and navigation that required teacher intervention at every step. Designing for "children with smartphones" was the wrong frame. We needed to design for low-spec devices, short attention spans, and zero onboarding support.

03

Design Approach

Every design decision traced back to one question: can a child complete the full creative loop on his/her own?

The core UX is built around Mitch Resnick's Creative Spiral , a iterative cycle of curiosity, making, reflecting, sharing.

Primary Experience : Creative Loop
Discover → Make → Reflect → Share → Feedback

Curiosity and peer feedback are the strongest motivators to spark young users' creative loop.

Facilitation Strategy : Interactive Emotional Scaffolding
  • Personalized content curation
  • Tailored questions
  • Chunk Instruction
  • Individualized AI feedback
Gamification Strategy
  • Narrative & Role Assignment : Users join a mission-driven world with characters and streak-based onboarding rewards that boost early engagement
  • Achievement : Dashboard displays badges, levels, and quest progress to help users track their growth and feel accomplishment
  • Creative Empowerment : Missions allow free expression, remixing, and profile decoration. AI-guided prompts scaffold hesitant users
  • Peer Interaction : Social feedback (e.g., emoji reactions, peer feed) builds relatedness and encourages iteration
04

3 design decisions
that shaped the product

Decision 01

Give every child a built-in facilitator.

One teacher can't guide twenty children through a new app at once. And children need a reason to care before they engage.

We introduced Ahha & Ohho, two characters who act as each child's personal guide. They hand out the first mission, walk through challenges, celebrate uploads, and suggest what to try next. Think Duolingo's owl, for creative learning.

Onboarding became a guided story instead of a content menu. By the final workshop sessions, children finished missions on their own and nobody had to teach the first run.

Decision 02

Close the creative loop inside one app.

In every school, the creative cycle broke at “share.” Teachers posted on Padlet, added context on YouTube, and made with other tools. The app was a starting point, never the full journey.

We redesigned the platform so the whole loop lives in one place. A child discovers a challenge, makes something, posts it, reflects, and gets feedback without ever leaving the app.

The feed is where the next loop begins. During testing, children made and shared 350+ projects inside the app. That work used to be scattered across Padlet, YouTube, and classroom walls.

Decision 03

Build the social layer as the product's core, not a feature.

Peer recognition drove repeat creation more than any content or reward. One teacher even chose a competitor for its community sharing.

We rebuilt the platform around the social layer. Reactions, peer visibility, and shared projects form the spine that every other feature connects to.

The feed now drives most return visits. Asked why they reopened the app, children gave the same answer: to see who reacted to my post.

01

Discover

Challenges are curated around interests rather than categories. When we watched children browse, they treated everything like a feed, so we designed discovery the same way.

02

Make

Each step is shown as a picture. In field tests, younger children skipped anything written in long text, so we stopped writing it.

03

Reflect

Reflection happens as a short conversation with Ahha and Ohho. We tried worksheet-style questions first. Children abandoned them.

04

Share

Publishing takes one tap from the personal gallery to the community feed. Every extra step before sharing cost us creators in testing.

05

Feedback

Reactions, comments, and small AI cues bring children back for the next cycle. Feedback arrives as celebration, the way children actually cheered each other on in workshops.

06

Design System

Children trust what responds. So every press, progress tick, and reward became a designed motion asset.

To keep the product playful at scale, I designed and built the motion layer of the AhhaOhho Design System. It holds 21 interactive Rive animations: buttons with built-in pressed states, data-bound progress meters, level-up celebrations, and onboarding scenes. Alongside them sits a 27-piece Lottie library of Ahha & Ohho character and feedback motions.

Interaction logic lives inside each .riv file’s state machine. The asset a designer builds is the same one that runs in the Flutter app, so nothing gets re-implemented and nothing drifts. Motion timing, color, and typography are all managed as designer-editable tokens in one repository.

Reflection

User tests showed that AhhaOhho could spark curiosity and creative agency in children, especially when they were able to make, document, and share their own interpretations. However, operating the product as a standalone service also revealed a key limitation: initial curiosity does not automatically lead to sustained engagement.

We learned that children need a clearer sense of purpose through missions, feedback, and growth. First-time users need a stronger onboarding path to understand the AhhaOhho universe and start creating without feeling lost. Facilitators also need practical tools that support group learning, not just individual exploration.

These learnings went into v1.0, launched in June 2026: a stronger social feedback layer, personalized AI curation, and facilitator tools that support repeat participation in classrooms as well as at home.
